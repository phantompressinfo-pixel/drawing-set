The one decision the comment turns on

The reviewer's operative sentence is “you need to pick one continuous barrier and stick to it. It needs to follow the thermal barrier.” Everything else — the table, the notes, the details you already have — is downstream of that single designation.

Your building makes the choice for you in two places. A conditioned wing runs west under a buried slab, and the two-story mass carries a clipped-gable roof over three bedrooms with a stair shaft running the full height beside it. A barrier taken at the interior finish would have to negotiate every top plate, every ceiling plane change, and that shaft. Taken at the exterior it is one unbroken line.

Below grade

Concrete + fully adhered waterproofing

The wall itself is the barrier. Seal form-tie holes, cold joints and every penetration; the membrane carries continuity across the slab-to-wall joint.

Above grade

Self-adhered WRB

Already specified in W3, W4, W10 and W11. Sits outboard of all framing and all insulation, so the Main and Upper rim joists are crossed without a single interior detail.

Roof

Membrane over the deck

Self-adhered underlayment at framed roofs; MM6125 on the slab at the green roof. Both ride outboard of the structure and lap straight onto the wall WRB.

Why the garage has to come out

Table R402.4.1.1 carries a dedicated garage separation row, which presumes the garage is outside the conditioned space; the reviewer's own sentence pairs “the thermal envelope AND the garage/dwelling separation” as two things, not one. As traced, the drawing answers neither cleanly: the barrier encloses a space with no insulation following it, which is the exact alignment failure the comment warns about.

The practical argument is blunter. A blower door test per RESNET/ICC 380 measures the enclosed conditioned volume. Sectional overhead doors seal against a rubber gasket on a concrete slab — they are among the leakiest assemblies in any house, and no amount of taping fixes that. Leaving the garage inside adds its volume and its doors to a test you must pass at 1.5 ACH50.

If the garage is genuinely intended to be heated and inside the envelope, that is a defensible design, but then say so explicitly on the sheet and carry it through: insulated overhead doors with listed U-factors, the garage envelope insulated to the same standard, and the garage volume included in the test. Confirm which it is before this goes back — it changes the drawing either way.

Do you need a detail for every keynote?

No. The reviewer's line about better detail is an incentive, not a requirement — and they offered their own sample details precisely so you would not have to draw the generic ones.

The working rule: draw a detail wherever the barrier changes plane or changes material. Everywhere else a keynote on the section is enough. A reviewer rejects undrawn transitions, not undrawn straight runs — nobody needs a detail to believe that a fully adhered WRB continues up a flat wall. By that test only five of the eight keynotes need a detail at all, and four of those are already on your sheets. What is genuinely missing is the grade transition, the garage separation, the mechanical chase and a window rough opening — and everything but the chase can be lifted from the reviewer's own samples.

6/A6.01 — buried slab to wall KEYNOTE 4 The one that fails the blower door

This is the reentrant corner: the exterior slab build-up on the left, the wall rising on the right, and a 90° inside corner between them that is permanently buried once the overburden goes on.

  1. The barrier line. One heavy continuous line from the slab waterproofing, through the corner, up the wall face, lapped onto the wall WRB. No break, no dotted segment.
  2. Cant strip or fillet at the inside corner, with reinforcing fabric. Self-adhered membranes bridge a hard 90° and crack there under movement. This is the most common failure at this exact condition and the cheapest thing on the page to fix.

  4. Termination bar and sealant. Where the membrane laps up the wall.
  5. Protection board. Placed over the membrane before any overburden goes on.
  6. Insulation continuity through the turn. Show the rigid insulation over the slab meeting the wall insulation with no gap. If the two do not touch at this corner, the barrier stops following the thermal barrier and you have handed the reviewer the exact objection the comment names.

Detail 6/A6.01 — annotation

Redrawn as a green roof. That changes the assembly substantially, and it changes the argument you make to the reviewer — for the better.

The Hydrotech assembly puts the waterproofing directly on the structural slab, and the R-50 closed-cell foam goes on the underside. So the barrier ends up above the slab and the insulation below it. On a framed roof that separation would be a finding. Here it is not, and the reason belongs on the sheet in one sentence: membrane, 8″ concrete and R-50 closed-cell foam are all air-impermeable and in continuous contact. No air-permeable layer occurs anywhere between the barrier and the insulation — which is the condition the “follow the thermal barrier” rule exists to prevent. State it and the objection never gets raised.

What the green roof changes

  1. The membrane sits on the slab, not above insulation. MM6125 is hot-applied directly to the structural deck — that is the only way it goes down. The barrier position is fixed at the top of the slab, and there is no choice to make.
  2. The insulation is on the underside. R-50 closed-cell foam below an 8″ slab, so the thermal line and the barrier line are separated by the slab. Note 1 on the detail is what makes that defensible rather than a finding.
  3. You need a vegetation-free zone at the wall. A 12″ gravel border at every wall and penetration. It keeps roots off the membrane termination and — the part the inspector cares about — keeps the termination visible instead of buried under soil.
  4. The root barrier gets its own turn-up. It runs up the wall behind the termination and stops above the growing medium, independent of the waterproofing turn-up.

Two blanks to fill: W__ on the wall assembly tag, and confirm the growing medium depth with landscape so the 8″ termination height above it is a real number rather than a nominal one.

The spray foam method

This is the reviewer's own sample sheet, which means six details on it can be lifted verbatim. But it also puts a question to you that the drawings currently answer the other way, and it needs settling before resubmittal.

First, a distinction worth holding onto

The city's sheet says housewrap because that is the common case. Your assemblies do not specify housewrap — they specify a self-adhered weather resistant barrier. Those are not the same product and not in the same class as air barriers. Housewrap is mechanically fastened and relies on taped laps; a fully adhered membrane is bonded to the substrate across its whole face. So “not just house paper” is right, and it is already true of what you have specified.

That leaves a genuine choice between two good materials, both of which are already in your walls: the cavity spray foam or the self-adhered membrane. The difference is not which seals better — both do. It is how many places the seal can fail.

The recommendation

Keep the self-adhered membrane as the designated barrier, and adopt the city's spray foam details as supplementary sealing. That is not a dodge — it is what the better builders actually do, and it costs you nothing you were not already buying. Three reasons:

The membrane is outboard of all framing, so the rim joists, plates and intersecting partitions never interrupt it. Your two air sealing diagrams are already traced that way, and both read as one unbroken line. Switching the designation to the cavity foam means retracing both diagrams so the line weaves through the framing, and revising roughly half the details — 4, 6, 7, 8 and 9 all change, because the plane the roof and the separation lap onto moves.

And it is the more forgiving answer at 1.5 ACH50. A cavity-foam barrier passes or fails on a sealant bead at every plate and stud pack, on three levels, in a house with this much framing complexity. Every one of those beads is concealed after close-in. The membrane is one plane you can walk and inspect before anything covers it.

If you would rather designate the foam, that is defensible and I will revise accordingly — but it should be a decision, not a drift, and it costs a redraw of both diagrams.

One note on A5.00 now reads backwards

Note 21 as placed says spray foam is not the air barrier. Read on site, that tells the framer and the insulator not to bother sealing the foam carefully — which is the opposite of what you want, whichever material carries the designation. Revise it, and add the city's sealing scope as its own note.
